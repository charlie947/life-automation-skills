# Review framework: how to turn a week into a plan

Five parts, always in this order: wins, open loops, this-week priorities, risks, calendar sanity check. The look-back (wins, open loops) is honest about what the connectors can and cannot prove. The look-forward (priorities, risks) is deliberately short. A weekly plan that lists fifteen things is a to-do list, not a plan.

## 1. Wins: what happened (proposed, not asserted)

The point is to close the week with a clear record of progress. The trap is claiming things happened because they were on the calendar.

- Draw candidate wins from last week's **completed** calendar events and from inbox threads that reached a clear resolution (a "thanks, done", a signed-off decision, a shipped deliverable).
- A calendar event is **scheduled intent, not confirmed outcome**. No connector reports whether a meeting happened, was attended, or achieved anything. So every win is written as *proposed, confirm this*, never as fact.
- Prefer wins with a trace: a thread that visibly closed, a document that was sent, a decision recorded in an email. These are stronger than "the 2pm sync was on the calendar".
- Keep it to real movement. Three genuine wins read better than ten routine meetings relabelled as achievements.
- Never invent a win. If last week looks thin, say so plainly. A quiet week is information, not a gap to paper over.

## 2. Open loops: what is still hanging

An open loop is something unfinished that the user is on the hook for. The connectors can surface candidates. They cannot prove intent.

- From Gmail, a loop is a thread where the user is plausibly the **awaited party**: they are not the last sender, or a direct question to them sits unanswered. Gmail has no native "awaiting my reply" state, so this is always an **inference**. Present it as one.
- From the calendar, a loop is a meeting that generated an action with no follow-up booked, or a recurring thread that stalled.
- Sort loops by who is blocked and for how long. A client waiting five days outranks a newsletter the user skimmed.
- Distinguish a loop (needs the user to act) from noise (CC'd, informational, already resolved). When unsure, leave it out and note the window you searched, rather than padding the list.
- An open loop is a thread that looks unanswered. It is not a commitment the user has made. Do not upgrade it into one.

## 3. This-week priorities: the hard cap

This is where the skill earns its keep. **Maximum three to five.** Fewer is fine. More is not allowed.

- A priority is a needle-mover: the two or three outcomes that, if they happen, make the week a success. It is not every task.
- Derive priorities from the open loops and the week ahead. If an open loop is important and time-bound, it is a candidate. If a big meeting needs preparation, that preparation is a candidate.
- Each priority gets one line on **why it matters this week** specifically. If you cannot say why it is this week rather than any week, it is probably not a priority.
- Do not manufacture priorities to reach five. An honest list of three beats a padded list of five. The cap is a ceiling, not a target.
- Where a priority maps to a real deadline or a booked meeting, name it. That is what makes the plan actionable rather than aspirational.

## 4. Risks: what could derail the week

Short, specific, each paired with a pre-empt. A risk with no counter-move is just worry.

- **Overload**: more committed hours than the week holds. Pre-empt: what to decline, move, or shorten.
- **Single point of failure**: one meeting or decision the whole week hinges on. Pre-empt: prep it first, have a fallback.
- **Collision**: a priority and a heavy meeting day landing together, so the priority gets no room. Pre-empt: move the focus block earlier.
- **Dependency**: a priority that needs someone else's input the user has not yet chased. Pre-empt: chase it Monday.
- Two or three real risks. Not a generic list. If the week genuinely looks clear, say so.

## 5. Calendar sanity check: does the plan survive contact with the diary

Cross the priorities against the actual calendar and surface the friction before Monday does.

- **No time for the priorities**: a stated priority with no focus block booked to do it. The most common failure. Flag it and suggest where a block fits.
- **Back-to-back stacks**: three or more meetings with no gap. Flag the day and suggest a break.
- **Zero focus time**: a day of meetings with no space for real work. Flag it.
- **Prep gap**: a meeting that needs preparation with no prep time scheduled. Flag it and suggest a slot.
- **Clash or double-book**: two events overlapping. Flag it, do not resolve it (this skill does not edit the calendar).
- Close with two counts: focus hours booked this week, and how many priorities actually have a block. If a priority has no block, the plan is not real yet, and the user should know before the week starts.

## The through-line

Look back honestly (proposed wins, inferred loops, never invented facts). Look forward narrowly (three to five priorities, named risks). Then pressure-test the forward plan against the diary. The value is not the list. It is the moment the sanity check shows a priority with nowhere to happen, while there is still time to fix it.
