# Brief structure: how to build a brief someone can act on

A good brief answers one question, puts the answer at the top, backs every material claim with a source, and is honest about what it could not pin down. It fits on a page. If it reads like a pile of search results, it has failed.

Build it in this order.

## 1. Question
One sharp question the brief exists to answer. Not a topic ("Acme Corp"), a question ("Is Acme a safe vendor to sign a 12-month contract with?"). Add two or three sub-questions underneath if it helps. If the ask was too vague to phrase this way, go back and get scope before researching.

## 2. Answer (up top)
Two to four sentences that answer the question, first, before any evidence. A busy reader should get the decision-useful takeaway without scrolling. Then one line of **confidence** (high / medium / low) and why. Confidence tracks how much the answer rests on verified sources versus single-source or thin evidence.

Do not bury the answer under the findings. The findings justify it, they do not replace it.

## 3. Key findings (each with a source)
The three to seven things that actually matter, each stated plainly and each carrying a source marker (`[S1]`, `[S2]`). Order by importance, not by when you found it.

Every finding is tagged by how solid it is:

- **Verified**: two or more independent, credible sources agree. State it flat.
- **Reported**: one credible source, not yet corroborated. Say so in the line ("reported, single source").
- **Rumour / unconfirmed**: speculation, a forum, an anonymous claim. Label it as such or leave it out. It never sits in the brief looking like a fact.

If a claim is load-bearing (a number, a legal status, an acquisition, a headcount), it needs a second independent source or it stays flagged as single-source. A claim you cannot source at all does not appear.

## 4. What's uncertain or disputed
The part most briefs skip, and the part that earns trust. List:

- Open questions the sources did not answer.
- Claims where sources conflict, with who says what.
- Things searched for but not found, written as "not found" or "could not verify", never quietly dropped or invented.

This section is where honesty lives. An empty version of it usually means the research was not adversarial enough.

## 5. Sources
Every source, numbered to match the markers, each with title, publisher, date, and URL, tagged **primary** (first-party: the company's own site, a filing, the person's own words, official docs) or **secondary** (someone reporting on it). Dates matter, a 2019 figure and a 2026 figure are different facts.

Only list sources you actually opened and read. A link that appeared in search but would not load is not a source, if its headline is worth noting, mark it "headline only, not verified" and keep it out of the fact base.

## Verification tiers at a glance

| Tag | Bar to clear | How it appears in the brief |
|-----|--------------|-----------------------------|
| Verified | 2+ independent credible sources | Stated as fact, with markers |
| Reported | 1 credible source, opened and read | Stated with "reported, single source" |
| Rumour / unconfirmed | Speculation, forum, anonymous, or unread | Flagged plainly, or cut |
| Not found | Searched, nothing credible | Named in "what's uncertain" |

## Rules that never bend

- **Cite everything material.** No source, no claim.
- **A result is not a read.** Opening the page is the bar for citing it.
- **Never state unverified as fact.** Flag it or cut it. Flagging is not a licence to include anything.
- **Never invent.** No placeholder stat, quote, or figure to fill a gap. "Not found" is a valid, useful answer.
- **Public data only** for people and companies. Flag sensitive or defamatory material rather than repeating it.
- **Answer first, evidence second, uncertainty owned.** That order is the whole point.
